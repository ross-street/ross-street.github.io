MACQUARIE ADVANCED SCIENCE: SPECIAL OPPORTUNITIES IN MATHEMATICS

 

"What are the advanced science programs at Macquarie?"

In 1992 Macquarie University introduced an advanced BSc program,focussing on mathematics and related areas. It was designed for students ofhigher mathematical ability to study mathematics or some other subject whichrequires a considerable level of mathematical expertise. This has runsuccessfully for 5 years and its UAC code has now changed to 300507. (The code used to be MQR, a label that is used as a nickname for the program.)

In 1996 there will also be a parallel program for students of asimilarly high calibre who wish to specialise in biological sciences. This has the UAC code 300508. Students interested in the biological programshould contact the School of Biological Sciences on 9850-8179.

 

"So I'll be doing only maths?"

Not at all. All Australian universities, in their BSc program,require you to get a good grounding in several sciences in first year andMacquarie is no exception. In later years you can concentrate heavily onmathematics, or you can balance it with complementary units from one or moreof the other sciences.

The flexibility of the Macquarie degree structure allows you to buildyour degree in a way that is most advantageous to your own interests andaspirations. Along with mathematics you might study astronomy, computing,electronics, physics, chemistry, biology, environmental studies, earthsciences, statistics or even music.

Many of the current Advanced Science students have chosen to major in mathematics and to pursue a research career in that direction. Others,while pursuing their mathematics into their third year, have chosen to majorinstead in astronomy, physics, computing or in some other area that usesmathematics deeply.

 

"Only science?"

Not necessarily, though of course the majority of your BSc doeshave to be in science subjects. But up to a quarter of your BSc can bein non-science areas. And for this you can choose from any part of theUniversity &emdash; from subjects normally associated with a BEc or BA degree.

 

"What degree do I get by doing the advanced BSc?"

If you follow the program the whole way you will graduate afterfour years with a BSc (Hons) degree and you will be ready to take off witha career in industry or to do a PhD and embark on a research career.Moreover if you achieve first-class honours you not only have a degree thatis quite distinctive but you will have a good chance of obtaining a postgraduate scholarship to support you while you undertake further studies.

 

"But if I don't want to follow this path all the way?"

No problem. Not only is it not necessary to major in mathematics, but you may in fact choose to leave mathematics at an earlier stage in order to concentrate on some science where mathematics has a lesser role. Since the requirements for your degree are the general ones that apply to the BSc you are not locked into doing any more mathematics than you want.

And if you don't want to do an honours year, preferring to finish up after three years, you simply take out the "ordinary" BSc degree (though with your academic transcript showing that you have done advanced units and have achieved superior grades, your "ordinary" BSc will show itself as being far from ordinary). Because the Advanced BSc Program is simply the normal BSc with additional opportunities, you will never be trapped in a program you find unsuitable.

 

"OK, but what are these extra opportunities?"

In Mathematics there are special advanced units that are usually available only to advanced students. In first-year there are two units designed specially for advanced students, in place of the normal ones.

In second and third year the advanced students do the same mathematics units as other students. These provide a solid grounding in ideas and techniques that pervade the whole subject. But as the content of these core units is to a large extent dictated by the need to service other sciences, they are not able to explore the more modern and exciting developments of the subject. For this reason there are a couple of additional units for the students in the Advanced Program.

Although these advanced units are more demanding than the ordinary ones, this is reflected in the very high proportion of Distinction (what used to be called A grades) awarded, so your grade-point-average (the university equivalent of the UAI) need not suffer.

For some other units you are permitted to enrol a year earlier than usual. This frees up your timetable in later years and gives you more opportunity to do the things you want.

In the long vacation at the end of each year we offer a number of vacation scholarships to advanced students for four to six weeks at $300 per week, tax free. These involve carrying out a small piece of research under the supervision of one of the research staff members. This provides the student with the opportunity of experiencing the world of research and also helps the staff to get to know the student better. In the 1994-95 vacation we awarded a total of sixteen of these scholarships for students at all three levels.

Finally, the group of students in the Advanced Program in Mathematics (often known as the MQR group because of the original UAC code) is in some ways a society of students with similar interests and abilities. We arrange a number of activities for the MQR students, such as team problem-solving competitions and mathematics hikes.

 

"What UAI will I need?"

You will need a UAI of at least 90, though in previous years the cut-off for the advanced program in mathematics has generally been around 92 to 93.

 

"I got a UAI over 98 wouldn't I be wasting it doing advanced science?"

There is a strange notion held by many students that one should opt for the course with the highest UAI that one can get into, otherwise "you're wasting it". This has led many students to make very unsuitable choices. Once you get above a UAI of 90, the cut-offs merely represent the equilibrium point of supply and demand and have nothing to do with the intrinsic difficulty of the course. Also, UAI's can also be a bit misleading. While it is true that a good UAI is an indicator for success there are many other factors. We have had many students in the program with UAI's of over 98 who have done very well. But we have noticed that a couple of students with very high "force-fed" UAI's have had difficulty in coming to terms with the self-motivation required for university study. Others with good, but less spectacular, UAI's have really blossomed in the tertiary environment.

 

"What about advanced science programs at other unis?"

You should check out what other universities can offer and compare it with what Macquarie's program can offer you. There is no such thing as one being better than another overall. But one can be better for you, and only you can make that decision.

What distinguishes the Macquarie Advanced Science Program in Mathematics is the central role it gives to mathematics. So if you intend to major in mathematics you should consider the benefit of a program which is specially focussed on your needs.

If you intend majoring in some other area but want to build that around a solid core of mathematics you should consider the way this strong focus can translate into improved job opportunities. Increasingly employers value graduates who have a strong background in mathematics in addition to their major. For example, although the market is currently flooded with computer science graduates, there is a shortage of computer science graduates who are strong in mathematics.

 

"I'm not doing 4-unit maths. Is that a problem?"

Entry to the mathematics-based advanced science program is based solely on the UAI. But it is assumed that you will have done Mathematics Extension 2 (4-unit) at the HSC, or its equivalent.

If you only have 3-unit, but have the required UAI, you will still be accepted. What it would mean is that in first-year you won't be able to take advantage of the extra opportunities. But from second year on you will be treated exactly the same as all other Advanced Science students.

 

"If I don't get in now, can I get in later?"

If you miss out on the advanced program but get accepted for the normal Macquarie BSc (UAC code 300517) and do really well in your first year mathematics, you will be invited into the Advanced Program in second year. We will also consider suitably well qualified students transferring from another university.

 

"Can I do an advanced BSc LLB or an advanced BSc (DipEd)?"

Because of the small number of students wanting it there is no specific UAC code for these combinations. You should apply for the BScLLB or BSc (DipEd) or whatever, using the normal UAC code. If you gain the required UAI for Advanced Science you can be included in the Advanced Program, even though you remain enrolled in the other degree. Just Professor Street.

 

"What about actuarial studies?"

Like the Advanced Science program, the Actuarial Studies program at Macquarie is highly regarded. However if you are trying to decide between them make sure you know what each of them involves. Talk to each department and make up your own mind.

Actuarial studies is very suitable for students who are good at mathematics but whose interests lie more in the world of commerce and who want a clearly defined career path. It is a very specialised course andnormally only includes one mathematics unit.

The Advanced Science program is more suitable for students who have a fundamental interest in mathematics itself, who are interested in the diversity of applications and who don't want to limit their career prospects to just one industry.

Overall the employment prospects for a mathematician are as good as for an actuary but the areas of employment are more diverse. The actuary generally earns more but the mathematician has more flexibilty in employment. Basically it all comes down to what sort of person you are.

Why not take an extra year and do both? There is a double degree BCom-ActStud BSc with Actuarial Studies and Advanced Mathematics forming a Coherent Study. The Area of Study code is ACT12.

 

"But isn't mathematics like a dead language?"

Mathematics may not be the oldest profession, but it is the most ancient of academic pursuits. All of the mathematics that's taught in schools today was around over 300 years ago. Even studying at university may only get you up to the beginning of the twentieth century. No wonder the subject has the reputation for being dusty, dry and dead.

What the general public don't realise is that important new discoveries are being made in mathematics at an ever increasing rate. In fact several problems that remained unsolved for over a hundred years have recently been solved. And whole new branches of mathematics are just opening up. These are exciting times for mathematics.

To give you some idea of the quantity of new mathematics being discovered today (and the quality is proportional), the publication Mathematical Reviews, publishes very short summaries of the more important papers in mathematics (new discoveries, not just reworking of old material) and each month's issue is almost as thick as one volume of the Sydney telephone book! (Actually, most mathematicians these days access it electronically.)

 

"Haven't computers made mathematicians redundant?"

Far from making mathematicians redundant, the advent of the computer has given mathematician powerful new tools. Perhaps more importantly, it has spawned whole new areas of mathematics by raising many fundamental questions that mathematicians are ideally equipped to answer. And important new advances in computing science have been built on new areas of mathematics. So mathematics and computing are not in competition. They are in an exciting partnership.

 

"Is there an advanced program in astronomy?"

Many students are attracted to Macquarie for the opportunity to study Astronomy at undergraduate level. There is no separate Advanced Program in astronomy simply because the program is designed to include all such mathematically-based subjects. There are many special opportunities available for students in the program, both in terms of course units and access to the University observatory. If you would like further details contact Dr Alan Vaughan on 9850-8904.

 

"Is Macquarie strong in mathematical research?"

Students are often reminded that they should choose a university, not because of its research (which they may never get to see) but on the quality of its teaching. This is excellent advice. (Actually, because of its small size, the advanced science group at Macquarie will come more into contact with research activities than is likely at the other universities.)

However, Macquarie need make no apology for the quality of its research. In terms of research grants awarded and international reputation Macquarie is now, for its size, one of the leading universities in the country in many, many subject areas. And one of these is mathematics. The mathematics department at Macquarie has two professors with distinguished international reputations, supported by many other academic staff members who are very active in research. We have centres of Number Theory, Analysis and Category Theory as well as individuals working in other areas, in particular, a new area of Applied Mathematics has been added. And there's a constant flow of international visitors to the department from all over the world who spend weeks or months working together with Macquarie academics.

 

But you should ignore all this! What's of most importance to you now is the quality of our undergraduate teaching! And that, as we are constantly reminded by ex-students and independent surveys, continues to be first class.
